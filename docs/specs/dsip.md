## Research Background

DSIP (Delta Sleep-Inducing Peptide) was first isolated from rabbit cerebral venous blood during electrically-induced sleep in the 1970s by Monnier and Schoenenberger. The isolated nonapeptide (Trp-Ala-Gly-Gly-Asp-Ala-Ser-Gly-Glu) was found to induce slow-wave (delta) sleep. Subsequent research expanded its profile beyond sleep: it modulates the HPA stress axis, produces opioid-independent analgesia, and influences circadian regulation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q1: What is Delta Sleep-Inducing Peptide (DSIP)?

DSIP is a naturally occurring nonapeptide first isolated from rabbit cerebral blood during induced slow-wave sleep. It promotes delta wave (0.5–4 Hz) EEG activity characteristic of deep, restorative sleep. Also modulates HPA stress axis and produces opioid-independent analgesia.

### Q2: How was DSIP discovered?

Discovered in the 1970s by Monnier and Schoenenberger, who electrically stimulated rabbit thalamus to induce sleep, collected cerebral venous blood, and isolated the factor that transferred sleep-inducing activity to recipient animals. Identified as the nonapeptide Trp-Ala-Gly-Gly-Asp-Ala-Ser-Gly-Glu.

### Q3: Does DSIP work through opioid receptors?

No — a key research distinction. DSIP produces analgesic effects but does not bind μ, δ, or κ opioid receptors. Its analgesic mechanism involves central serotonin and GABA pathways. This opioid-independent pain modulation makes it a unique research tool.

### Q4: How does DSIP compare to melatonin for sleep research?

Melatonin primarily regulates sleep timing (circadian phase shifting). DSIP primarily affects sleep depth (delta wave promotion). Different aspects of sleep physiology — can be studied in complementary protocols.
